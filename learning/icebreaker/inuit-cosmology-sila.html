Inuit Cosmology - Sila

An icebreaker

As the tradition holds, Sila is not just weather but a conscious force running through air, breath, and intelligence throughout the cosmos. Elders teach that how a person lives can affect Sila's balance, making respect for it a form of right conduct.

Worth knowing · 1 of 5
What is Sila, in the Inuit tradition?
As the tradition holds, Sila names a force running through weather, breath, and thought at once — not a god to worship, but a conscious power that responds to how people live and treat the world around them.
Worth knowing · 2 of 5
Is Sila just another word for 'the weather god'?
No — the tradition holds that flattening Sila into a single deity misses the point. It names something closer to the intelligence running through wind, sky, and mind together, present everywhere rather than located in one figure.
Worth knowing · 3 of 5
How did outsiders first record this concept?
An early twentieth-century expedition recorded an elder describing a great power that communicates through storms and the fury of the sea when people show no reverence for their daily food and the life around them.
Worth knowing · 4 of 5
Does a person's own behavior affect it?
As the tradition holds, yes — living carelessly or without respect can disturb its balance, while right conduct keeps it calm. The relationship runs both ways, understood as consequence rather than punishment.
Worth knowing · 5 of 5
Is this belief still held today?
Many Inuit writers and elders still describe it as central to how the world is understood, and teach younger generations to read weather and land as expressions of a wider, attentive intelligence.
Words to know
SilaThe Inuit concept naming weather, breath, intelligence, and cosmic order as a single interconnected force.
AngakkuqAn Inuit shaman or spiritual specialist, traditionally understood to communicate with Sila and other powers.
NunaLand or earth, often held together with Sila in Inuit thought as the ground of shared existence.

A voice · 1 of 4
Najagneq · Inuit angakkuq interviewed in the 1920s

Speaking with a visiting expedition in the early 1920s, he described Sila as a great spirit sustaining weather and all life, communicating through storms and the sea's fury whenever people showed no reverence for their food and daily life.

A voice · 2 of 4
Knud Rasmussen · Greenlandic-Danish ethnographer, Fifth Thule Expedition

Leading an expedition across the Arctic from 1921 to 1924, he recorded Najagneq's account of Sila and published it in the Report of the Fifth Thule Expedition, treating the testimony as serious cosmology rather than folklore.

A voice · 3 of 4
Rachel Qitsualik · Inuk writer on Inuit cosmology

In a widely read 1998 essay, she described Sila as a concept at once intellectual, environmental and spiritual, arguing it may be the most important idea in classical Inuit thought and that “Great Spirit” mistranslates it.

A voice · 4 of 4
Jarich Oosten · Anthropologist who studied Inuit shamanism

Together with a research partner, he documented how understandings of Sila and related spiritual forces persisted and changed through the twentieth century, drawing on oral testimony collected directly from Inuit elders in Nunavut.
