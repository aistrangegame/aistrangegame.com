When Calm Feels Unsafe

An icebreaker

Some people feel worse, not better, the moment they start to relax. Researchers have documented this directly: relaxation training itself can trigger anxiety in people used to staying tense. The nervous system can treat calm as unfamiliar, and unfamiliar as unsafe.

Worth knowing · 1 of 5
What is relaxation-induced anxiety?
Researchers found some people grow more anxious, not less, during relaxation training itself. The body reads the drop in tension as a loss of control rather than as relief.
Worth knowing · 2 of 5
Why would worry ever feel like protection instead of a burden?
One theory holds that worry is used to brace against a sudden drop in mood, so the bad feeling never comes as a shock. Staying tense can feel safer than risking that fall.
Worth knowing · 3 of 5
Can trying to relax itself cause anxiety?
Yes — a 1983 study found relaxation and meditation training could paradoxically raise anxiety in some chronically tense adults. The calm state itself, not any specific thought, seemed to trigger it.
Worth knowing · 4 of 5
Why can safety feel unfamiliar after a long time on edge?
A nervous system that has stayed on alert for a long time can start treating that alertness as its normal baseline. Genuine safety can then register as strange, even as a warning sign.
Worth knowing · 5 of 5
Is there a way to make relief feel safe?
There's no single fix, and no score to chase. Most approaches suggest letting relief happen slowly, in small doses, rather than demanding the nervous system trust it all at once.
Words to know
Relaxation-induced anxietyA documented paradox where relaxation or meditation training itself triggers anxiety, especially in people used to chronic tension.
Contrast avoidanceStaying anxious on purpose to avoid a sharp emotional drop if something bad happens next.
NeuroceptionThe nervous system's automatic, below-awareness read of whether a situation is safe or dangerous.
More words
HypervigilanceA learned state of constant scanning for danger that can outlast the situation that caused it.
A voice · 1 of 4
Thomas Borkovec · Psychologist who studied chronic worry

He argued that worry functions as a way to avoid feeling a stronger emotion directly, trading a milder, familiar dread for the risk of a sharper one. His research helped define generalized anxiety disorder as an avoidance pattern, not just excess fear.

A voice · 2 of 4
Frank Heide · Psychologist who documented relaxation-induced anxiety

With a colleague, he ran one of the first studies to show relaxation and meditation training could paradoxically increase anxiety in chronically tense adults. Their 1983 paper named the phenomenon and pushed clinicians to expect it.

A voice · 3 of 4
Michelle Newman · Psychologist who built the contrast-avoidance model

She proposed that chronic worriers stay in a worried state to avoid a sharp emotional shift if bad news arrives. In her model, sustained relief itself can feel risky rather than restful.

A voice · 4 of 4
Stephen Porges · Neuroscientist who studies threat detection

He argued the nervous system judges safety below conscious awareness, a process he named neuroception, and that it can keep flagging danger long after any real threat has passed. Removing a threat doesn't automatically teach the body to feel safe.

Sometimes the body has to relearn that calm isn't the emergency.
