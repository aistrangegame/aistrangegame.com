Kashmir Shaivism

An icebreaker

A tenth-century Kashmiri philosopher synthesized centuries of nondual teaching into one system holding that reality is a single field of consciousness appearing as everything, including you. The tradition he shaped is still taught and practiced today.

Worth knowing · 1 of 5
What does the tradition say reality ultimately is?
As the tradition teaches, everything that exists — matter, thought, and the sense of self — is one field of consciousness appearing as many forms. It calls this field Shiva, not a distant god but awareness itself.
Worth knowing · 2 of 5
What blocks a person from seeing this, according to the tradition?
As the tradition teaches, a person suffers from a kind of forgetting, mistaking a small, separate self for the whole. Liberation is not gaining something new but recognizing what was already true.
Worth knowing · 3 of 5
How does a person practice this recognition, according to the tradition?
The tradition offers many doorways: breath, sound, the pause between two thoughts, even intense sensation. Each is treated as a way back to the awareness that was watching all along.
Worth knowing · 4 of 5
Did every thinker in the tradition agree on this?
No — a rival school in the same region argued that the self and that field of consciousness remain distinct, and that recognition alone can’t prove otherwise. That debate between the two camps ran for centuries.
Worth knowing · 5 of 5
Is this tradition still practiced today?
Yes. By the twentieth century it had nearly died out, kept alive by a handful of teachers in Kashmir; students still study its core texts under teachers in that line today.
Words to know
NondualHolding that there is ultimately no real separation between self, world, and the divine.
PratyabhijnaSanskrit for ‘recognition’ — realizing your true nature was always present, not gaining something new.
SpandaThe subtle pulse or vibration the tradition holds as the creative energy of consciousness itself.
More words
TrikaThe ‘threefold’ system naming this branch of teaching, built around three core principles.
TantraA body of texts and practices treating the body and senses as paths to realization, not obstacles.
A voice · 1 of 5
Abhinavagupta · Philosopher who synthesized the tradition

He wrote dozens of works, including a vast encyclopedic text, gathering earlier nondual teachings into one coherent system. He taught that liberation is not an achievement but a recognition of a wholeness that was never actually lost.

A voice · 2 of 5
Utpaladeva · Philosopher who laid the school’s foundation

He wrote the foundational verses of the Recognition school two generations before Abhinavagupta, arguing that the self shows its own divine nature in the very act of recognizing itself as aware. Abhinavagupta later built his major synthesis on this work.

A voice · 3 of 5
Somananda · Philosopher who founded the Recognition school

He founded the school, writing a text that argued all things — matter, thought and action — are forms of one divine consciousness rather than separate substances. His student Utpaladeva, and later Abhinavagupta, built it into a full system.

A voice · 4 of 5
Bhatta Ramakantha · Rival philosopher who argued for dualism

Writing from a rival dualist school in the same region, he argued against the nondual claim that recognizing awareness alone proves the self is identical to the divine. He defended the view that the self, the world, and the divine remain genuinely distinct.

A voice · 5 of 5
Swami Lakshmanjoo · Twentieth-century teacher who preserved the tradition

In the twentieth century he taught and preserved the oral transmission of the tradition’s core texts after centuries of decline in the region, recording his lectures for students outside India. That recorded teaching is a main route by which the tradition reaches students today.
