The Simulation Hypothesis

An icebreaker

A philosopher argued in 2003 that at least one must be true: civilizations rarely reach the stage of running realistic ancestor-simulations, those that reach it choose not to, or we are almost certainly living in one. He accepts the logic, not the conclusion.

Worth knowing · 1 of 5
What exactly is the trilemma he proposed?
He argued that at least one of three claims must hold: almost no civilizations reach the technology to run realistic ancestor-simulations, those that can choose not to run them, or we are almost certainly inside one, since simulated minds would then vastly outnumber real ones.
Worth knowing · 2 of 5
Does he personally believe we're in a simulation?
He hasn't claimed it. He has said he accepts the logical structure of the trilemma, that one of the three options is true, but not which one, and has resisted being read as endorsing the simulation itself.
Worth knowing · 3 of 5
What's the main mathematical criticism of the argument?
One astronomer re-scored the argument with Bayesian statistics, weighing the options evenly. He found the odds of living in a simulation come out just under fifty-fifty, slightly favoring an unsimulated reality.
Worth knowing · 4 of 5
What's the physical argument against it being possible at all?
Some physicists argue that simulating a universe this large and detailed from within itself runs into hard energy limits, since processing information carries an unavoidable minimum energy cost that any simulator would still be bound by.
Worth knowing · 5 of 5
Has anyone argued we shouldn't even try to test it?
Yes. One philosopher argued in a 2019 opinion piece that any experiment designed to reveal we're in a simulation could be catastrophically risky, reasoning that a simulator discovering it had been detected might simply shut the simulation down.
Words to know
Simulation argumentHis 2003 trilemma about ancestor-simulations, distinct from the simulation hypothesis itself, which is only one of its three branches.
Posthuman civilizationHis term for a civilization advanced enough to run computer simulations detailed enough to contain conscious minds.
Ancestor-simulationA hypothetical detailed simulation of a civilization's own past, populated with simulated people who don't know they're simulated.
More words
Bayesian priorA starting probability assigned to a claim before new evidence is factored in, used by later critics to re-score his argument.
A voice · 1 of 4
Nick Bostrom · Philosopher who proposed the trilemma

In his 2003 paper he argued that at least one of three propositions must be true about advanced civilizations and simulation, and stated he accepts that logical structure while remaining agnostic about which of the three options actually holds.

A voice · 2 of 4
David Kipping · Astronomer who re-scored the argument statistically

In 2020 he applied Bayesian statistics to the trilemma, weighing its options evenly, and found the odds of living in a simulation come out just under fifty-fifty, slightly favoring an unsimulated reality.

A voice · 3 of 4
Franco Vazza · Astrophysicist who argued it's physically implausible

He has argued it would be physically impossible to simulate a meaningful portion of the universe from within itself, since processing information carries an unavoidable minimum energy cost that no simulating system could escape.

A voice · 4 of 4
Preston Greene · Philosopher who argued against testing it

In a 2019 opinion piece he argued that experiments aimed at revealing whether this is a simulation could be extremely dangerous, reasoning that if detected, whoever runs the simulation might end it, and concluded the risk wasn't worth taking.

He proposed the puzzle. He never claimed to have solved it.
