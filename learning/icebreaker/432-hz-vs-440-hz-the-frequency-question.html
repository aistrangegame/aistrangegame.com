432 Hz vs 440 Hz

An icebreaker

440 Hz became the international tuning standard in 1955 after decades of drift toward it, not from any special formula. A small study using music tuned to 432 Hz found modest heart-rate changes, but no controlled research shows either number 'heals' anything.

Worth knowing · 1 of 5
Where did 440 Hz actually come from?
American orchestras and instrument makers had mostly converged on it by the mid-1920s, and an international conference recommended it in 1939. The International Organization for Standardization formally adopted 440 Hz in 1955, as a workable compromise, not a scientific discovery.
Worth knowing · 2 of 5
Is it true 440 Hz was imposed by the Nazis?
No credible historical evidence supports that. The claim was popularized decades later by a political fringe group promoting a rival lower pitch, then spread further online. Standardization actually followed years of independent industry drift, mostly within the United States.
Worth knowing · 3 of 5
What does the actual research on 432 Hz say?
One small crossover study found slightly lower heart rate and blood pressure after listening to movie music tuned to 432 Hz versus 440 Hz. That is a modest relaxation effect, not evidence of healing, DNA repair, or cosmic resonance.
Worth knowing · 4 of 5
Did any famous composer prefer a lower pitch?
A leading Italian opera composer argued in the 1880s for a lower concert pitch than the rising one of his day, to spare singers' voices. Advocates of 432 Hz now name their preferred pitch after him.
Worth knowing · 5 of 5
Why does a tuning debate attract conspiracy theories?
A round, appealing number like 432 invites claims about hidden universal patterns, while a committee-negotiated standard like 440 can feel arbitrary or imposed from outside. The gap between those feelings and the boring bureaucratic history is where the stories grow.

A voice · 2 of 3
Johann Heinrich Scheibler · Instrument maker who proposed 440 Hz

In 1834 he presented pitch measurements from a device he built, called a tonometer, recommending 440 Hz to a meeting of German scientists. It was one recommendation among several competing standards used across nineteenth-century Europe.

A voice · 3 of 3
Sandra Garrido · Music psychologist who reviewed the wellbeing claims

In a 2026 article she found little scientific support for any special wellbeing effect of 432 Hz, noting the main study was small and non-randomised, and argued that music's effects depend more on how a listener perceives it than on any single frequency.
