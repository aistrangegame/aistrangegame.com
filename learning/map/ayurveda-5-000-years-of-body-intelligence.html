Ayurveda: The Five Elements

Space, air, fire, water, earth · a map

Ayurveda's own account of the body: five elements said to combine into three governing forces, first written down in Sanskrit texts roughly two thousand years ago.

How to read it
The five elements are held here as the tradition's own map, not as chemistry. Its classical texts, the Caraka and Suśruta Saṃhitās, took shape between roughly the last centuries BCE and the early centuries CE. Ayurvedic products can interact with medicines and some have been found to contain heavy metals — check with a doctor.

Space

ākāśa · pairs with air into vata

Where everything else finds room to exist.
Ākāśa is space itself — the tradition's first element, the field of gap and distance that lets the other four exist and move within it. Paired with air, the tradition says, it forms vata, the dosha of motion and change.
2 of 5

Air

vāyu · with space, completes vata

Movement itself, restless and always changing.
Vāyu is air, the element of movement — breath, nerve impulse, the pulse of change through the body. Joined with space, the tradition holds, it completes vata, the dosha said to govern movement: breath, circulation, and the body's impulses.
3 of 5

Fire

agni (tejas) · with water, forms pitta

Heat that transforms one thing into another.
Agni, sometimes called tejas, is fire — the element of transformation, heat, and digestion in its widest sense. Paired with water, the tradition says, it forms pitta, the dosha linked to metabolism, body temperature, and how the body processes what it takes in.
4 of 5

Water

jala (āpa) · in both pitta and kapha

The one element the tradition gives to two.
Jala, or āpa, is water — the element the tradition places in two doshas at once. With fire it forms half of pitta; with earth, further on, it forms half of kapha too, said to carry both digestion's fluids and the body's lubrication.
5 of 5

Earth

pṛthvī · with water, forms kapha

Structure, weight, and what holds together.
Pṛthvī is earth, the element of structure, weight, and solidity — bone, muscle, and tissue in the classical scheme. Paired with water, the tradition says, it forms kapha, the dosha of stability and steady growth, and the last of the five to be named.
