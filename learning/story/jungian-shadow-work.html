Jungian Shadow Work

A storybook in 7 pages

Carl Jung named the shadow: the parts of a person pushed out of awareness because they don't fit who we think we are. What began as clinical theory is now a self-help practice online, and the two don't always agree.

1 of 7

The Part You Don't Claim

Jung named it after the shape it casts.

In his 1951 book Aion, the psychiatrist Carl Jung described the shadow as the unconscious, disowned side of a person's personality: not evil by nature, just unacknowledged. He wrote that becoming aware of it takes real moral effort, since most of what's in there was pushed out precisely because it was too uncomfortable to look at directly.

Seeing It in Someone Else

What we can't own, we hand to others.

Jung described projection as the mechanism by which the shadow usually shows itself: a trait we can't stand in ourselves gets unconsciously placed onto someone else, who then seems to deserve all our irritation. The people who annoy us most, in this account, are often carrying something we haven't yet recognized as our own.

Integration, Not Defeat

The goal was never to erase it.

For Jung, the point of facing the shadow wasn't to destroy it but to integrate it: to become a more whole person by owning the parts once disowned, under the guidance of trained analysis. He didn't describe this as quick or comfortable. It was slow work, done with a professional, over years.

Sixty-Five Essays

A 1991 book carried the idea further.

In 1991, Jungian therapists Connie Zweig and Jeremiah Abrams edited 'Meeting the Shadow,' a collection of essays applying the concept to relationships, work, and creativity. It sold widely and helped move the term out of clinical rooms and into general conversation: the first big step toward what would later become 'shadow work.'

A Prompt on a Screen

Now it fits in a short video.

On social media today, shadow work usually means a journaling prompt: what do you avoid, resent, or deny in yourself? It's framed as approachable self-help, no analyst, no years, no trained guide required. Many of the people posting it have no clinical training in what they're describing.

6 of 7

Not a Substitute

If something heavy comes up, say so out loud.

A therapist trained in Jung's work, Imi Lo, has said she welcomes the wider interest but worries the online version gets distorted through repetition. This isn't therapy, and a journaling prompt isn't a diagnosis or a treatment. If facing something in yourself brings up more than you can carry alone, that's worth telling a real, trained person, not just a page.

The Part You Meet

What's disowned doesn't need an audience.

A prompt can start something a page can't finish. What Jung asked of his patients, over years, with someone trained to hold it, a video can only gesture toward. The part of you pushed into shadow doesn't need to perform. It needs attention, and sometimes it needs more than you alone.

7
Before you go
If you want to talk about this with someone:
  • Who in your life bothers you in a way that feels bigger than they deserve?
  • Is there a part of yourself you've never let anyone see?
  • Would you want a guide for this, or is doing it alone enough?
