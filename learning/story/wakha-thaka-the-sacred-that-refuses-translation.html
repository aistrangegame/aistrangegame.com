Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka

Lakota sacred concept · a storybook in 4 pages

Lakota speakers call it Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka — closer to Great Mystery than the usual English gloss, Great Spirit, and never fully carried by either.

1 of 4

Not one god

Closer to Mystery.

English translations often render Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka as 'Great Spirit,' as if it named a single being. Lakota scholars have said 'Great Mystery' comes closer — naming the sacred itself, not one figure among others.

1
2 of 4

Sixteen powers

Many, and one.

One early recorder, working among Lakota elders from 1896 to 1914, documented them describing it as sixteen related sacred powers, not a single god. Scholars still weigh how literally that count was meant.

2
3 of 4

What gets lost

A word, forced smaller.

Lakota writers have said that forcing the term into borrowed English — 'spirit,' 'god' — shrinks a worldview into a single word it was never meant to hold.

3
4 of 4

Left untranslated

Some words, you learn.

One Lakota-language teacher taught for decades that some words carry no real English equivalent and lose meaning the moment they are forced into one — better learned in Lakota than approximated in translation.

4
Before you go
If you want to talk about this with someone:
  • What words in your own life resist being translated into something smaller?
  • Can respect for a word mean leaving it exactly as it is?
Where this comes from
As James R. Walker recorded from Lakota elders (1896–1914); anthropologist Raymond DeMallie; and Lakota writers Vine Deloria Jr. and Albert White Hat Sr., all reported speech, verified publicly.
