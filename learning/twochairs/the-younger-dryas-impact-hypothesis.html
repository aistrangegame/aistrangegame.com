The Younger Dryas Impact Question

Two chairs

In 2007, researchers proposed that comet fragments struck Earth about 12,900 years ago, triggering sudden cold and mass extinction. One chair trusts the sediment evidence. The other says it never adds up to an impact.

The question

Around 12,900 years ago, Earth's climate suddenly plunged toward ice-age cold, and many large ice-age animals went extinct soon after. In 2007, physicist Richard Firestone and colleagues proposed that fragments of a disintegrating comet struck or exploded overhead, pointing to unusual materials in sediment layers worldwide. Other scientists reviewing the same evidence say it doesn't add up to an impact.

Round 1
The chair that trusts the sedimentIn 2007, Richard Firestone and colleagues, including geologist James Kennett, found tiny magnetic spherules and nanodiamonds in sediment from that exact period, across multiple continents.
The chair that says no craterA 2023 review led by archaeologist Vance Holliday called the case unconvincing — no impact crater from the period has ever been found, anywhere.
Round 2
The chair that trusts the sedimentImpacts don't always leave a crater — an airburst that explodes above the ground can scatter debris without ever cratering the surface below it.
The chair that says no craterMaybe, but Holliday's review also found the proposed evidence hasn't converged into one coherent scenario — different sites, different signals, no clean match.
Round 3
The chair that trusts the sedimentKennett and Firestone published a detailed rebuttal, defending the evidence as reproduced at dozens of sites — that's not nothing, even if it isn't proof.
The chair that says no craterDozens of sites and still no agreement, years later, on what actually happened — that's not a settled case, it's an open argument.

Held open

Sediment layers from around 12,900 years ago really do contain unusual materials at many sites. Whether they mean a comet struck, or something more ordinary, is still disputed between the researchers who found them and the ones who reviewed the find.

The layer is real. What fell, if anything, is still argued.
