The Schumann Resonance

Two chairs

In 1952, a physicist predicted Earth's atmosphere would ring at about 7.83 Hz. It does. One chair holds only the confirmed physics. The other holds what believers say it does for the body.

The question

Between Earth's surface and its ionosphere sits a natural electrical cavity, kept ringing by lightning striking somewhere on the planet at every moment. In 1952, physicist Winfried Otto Schumann calculated it should resonate near 7.83 Hz; measurements later confirmed it. Some now call this 'Earth's heartbeat,' tied to human brainwaves and wellbeing. Does the physics support that?

Round 1
The chair that holds the feelingPeople describe feeling calmer, clearer, more 'grounded' near recordings of this frequency — practitioners call it the planet's own heartbeat, always present, always low.
The chair that holds the measurementThe resonance is real physics, confirmed since 1960 — but no peer-reviewed study has shown it affects human mood, health or brainwaves directly.
Round 2
The chair that holds the feelingHuman brainwaves do sit in a similar low range as the resonance. That overlap is why the comparison caught on in the first place.
The chair that holds the measurementA shared range of numbers isn't a mechanism. Two things can sit near the same frequency without one causing anything in the other.
Round 3
The chair that holds the feelingSome believers argue that modern electromagnetic noise now drowns out a signal humans once lived inside all the time.
The chair that holds the measurementThat is a claim about exposure, and it is separate from whether the resonance changes how anyone feels. Keep the two apart.

Held open

The resonance itself is confirmed physics, measured since 1960. Claims that it governs mood, energy or consciousness are unproven and belong to the people who make them. If you're changing how you sleep or feel for health reasons, talk to a doctor.
