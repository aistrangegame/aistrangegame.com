Gerald Pollack

b. 1940 · A portrait

A University of Washington bioengineering professor built a mainstream research career, then argued that water next to certain surfaces forms an ordered fourth phase with sweeping effects in living cells.

How to read it

The line above is how his claims were received — by the scientists whose job was to doubt them. It rises as a claim is tested and accepted, and the room brightens with it. It is not a verdict on truth: some accepted ideas are later overturned, and some rejected ones come back. A hollow point is a claim still open.

1 of 5 · 1968–90 · proposed

A career, then a first break

He earned a bioengineering PhD from the University of Pennsylvania in 1968 and built a conventional career in cell biology and muscle contraction. His 1990 book proposed muscle fibers contract by a polymer-gel phase change, not the standard sliding-filament model. Muscle physiologists did not adopt it.

2 of 5 · 2003 · tested

The exclusion zone

With Jin-Ming Zheng, he published evidence in Physical Review E that water next to certain gel surfaces forms a wide zone excluding dissolved particles. Other laboratories later independently confirmed the exclusion effect itself, even as its underlying cause stayed disputed.

3 of 5 · 2012–13 · proposed

A fourth phase, named

After winning the Prigogine Medal for thermodynamics in 2012, he argued in his 2013 book that the exclusion-zone layer is a distinct, ordered phase of water underlying cell hydration, blood flow, and how living cells generate usable energy.

4 of 5 · 2020 · rejected

The critical review

Physicist Daniel Elton and colleagues published a detailed review confirming the exclusion effect is real but arguing ordinary particle physics explains it better than a new phase of water, calling several of the broader biological claims physically implausible.

5 of 5 · today · proposed

Where it stands

Bottled "structured water" products sell on the strength of the idea, well ahead of any settled science. The exclusion effect is accepted as a real, replicated phenomenon; water science and biology have not accepted it as a fourth phase of water.

The exclusion-zone effect is real. Whether it is a new phase of water isn't.
What his claim leaves open is whether an exclusion-zone layer counts as a genuinely new phase of water, or ordinary physics under an unfamiliar name.
