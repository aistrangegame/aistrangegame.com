Hawaiian Ho'oponopono — Cleaning the Lineage

One practice, told two ways

The teaching
In traditional Hawaiian practice, ho'oponopono is a family gathering, often led by an elder, where a conflict is named and made right between the people actually involved. In the 1970s, one kahuna adapted it into a solo, inward practice — the popular self-help version most people now know by that name.
Why this is hard
Ho'oponopono means, roughly, to make right. In its older form, it's communal: family members sit together, name what went wrong, and work it through, usually guided by an elder. Morrnah Simeona reshaped it in the 1970s into something a single person could do alone, aimed inward rather than at a room of relatives.

The practice
If you try the popular phrases — I'm sorry, please forgive me, thank you, I love you — notice which version you're actually doing: something aimed at another person, or something aimed only at yourself. Both are real; they aren't interchangeable.
One name holds two practices now. Knowing which one you're doing is worth something.
