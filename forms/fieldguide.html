When It Feels Like a Panic Attack

A field guide

A lookup for the moment itself — what to do first, then why it works — and what the cycle looks like from the outside.

Which is it?

1 of 3
When
Your heart is racing and you think something is medically wrong
Do
Sit or lean on something. You are not going to fall.
Make the out-breath longer than the in-breath. Six slow ones.
Name five things you can see. Out loud if you can.
Because
A panic surge peaks and passes on its own within minutes. The body cannot sustain it. Everything above shortens the peak; nothing above is required for it to end. If chest pain is new, crushing, or spreads to the arm or jaw, that is a different situation — call emergency services.
2 of 3
When
You feel it starting and want to stop it
Do
Don't fight it. Fighting is fuel.
Say: this is a false alarm and it will pass.
Do something with your hands — cold water, a wall, the ground under your feet.
Because
The cycle is: a sensation → a frightening thought about the sensation → more adrenaline → a bigger sensation. The thought is the only link you can change. Not by arguing with it — by declining to believe it for two minutes.
3 of 3
When
It passed, and now you're afraid of the next one
Do
Write down what happened and how long it actually lasted.
Do the ordinary thing you were about to do before it started.
Tell one person, plainly.
Because
Fear of the next attack is what turns a bad afternoon into a pattern. Avoiding the place or the activity teaches the body the alarm was right. Returning teaches it the alarm was wrong. That teaching is the whole recovery.
The alarm is real. The fire is not.
If this is happening often, that is exactly what a therapist is for. This page is a hand on the shoulder, not a treatment.
