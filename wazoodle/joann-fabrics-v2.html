The Reality

Three things every fabric buyer needs to understand now.

The closure of Joann isn't just a retail story. It's the end of a system — and the beginning of a question most fabric buyers haven't asked yet.

01
The Joann Reality
800 stores didn't close because of bad luck. They closed because the model was designed to break.

In 2011, private equity firm Leonard Green & Partners took Joann private in a $1.6 billion leveraged buyout. Of that, $1.15 billion was borrowed money — debt that Joann itself had to carry. A company that had zero debt in 2010 was suddenly spending over $100 million per year on interest payments alone.

That debt reshaped every decision. Staffing was cut. Quality suffered. Customer service — the thing that made a fabric store a fabric store — disappeared. When COVID hit and crafting boomed, it looked like a lifeline. Revenue surged to $1.9 billion. But it was a mirage. The pandemic demand faded. The debt didn't.

The second bankruptcy came in January 2025. Suppliers had already started pulling back — deliveries became unpredictable, entire product lines were discontinued. By May 30, 2025, the last Joann store was gone. 19,000 jobs. 80 years. Dissolved.

This wasn't a failure of crafting. It was a failure of a financial model that prioritized extraction over the people it served.

02
The Supply Chain Reality
Switching to another online retailer? You're probably buying from the same fragile overseas pipeline Joann used.

Here's what most fabric buyers don't realize: the online alternatives they turned to after Joann started struggling source from the same overseas manufacturers. The supply chain that failed Joann is the same supply chain feeding most of the online fabric marketplace.

You go one path or the other — you end up at the same place. The same tariff vulnerability. The same quality inconsistencies. The same question every time you place an order: will this actually arrive? Will it be what I expected?

Tariffs on imported textiles continue to increase. Supply chain disruptions haven't stopped — they've become the new normal. Every buyer relying on overseas-sourced fabric is one container delay or policy change away from the same disruption that took down Joann.

The lateral move — from a physical store to an online store that uses the same supply chain — doesn't solve the structural problem. It only changes the screen you're looking at when the disruption hits.

03
The American-Made Reality
You've been told domestic manufacturing can't compete. What if that belief is the only thing that was ever imported?

Since the 1980s, the textile industry moved offshore. The assumption calcified: American-made fabric is too expensive, too limited, too niche to matter. Joann reinforced that assumption for decades — importing cheap fabric from overseas and selling it at markup.
