What are you staying high enough to not feel?
Habits (Stay High) is a body trying every way it can find to turn its own volume down around the empty room where someone used to be — reaching, filling, crowding the small hours, staying blurred enough to keep the absence at arm's length — while the Hippie Sabotage remix drags the whole song underwater to a slow 2.5 Hz tidal pulse, until the song about staying numb reveals itself as the truest cry for rest in the room: a water body discovering that what it chased through every blur was never oblivion, only the deep still floor where grief is finally safe to be felt.

The Words

Once the ache of one particular person is subtracted from it, this is what the song is: a body that has discovered it can survive an unbearable feeling by turning its own volume down, and has mistaken that trick for a life. Everything it reaches for — the food, the emptiness, the loud rooms full of strangers, the pretend world it stays inside — is one gesture repeated, the gesture of not-feeling, performed over and over because the alternative is a grief that feels like it would end you. But numbing was never a way out of the feeling; it was only the feeling with the lights off, still there, still waiting in the dark. What the body actually wants, underneath every blur, is not to feel nothing forever — it is to rest. To stop bracing. To lie all the way down in the deep and be held while the loss moves through, instead of standing guard at the door of it forever. The song is staying high in order not to feel. What it is really asking for is somewhere safe enough to finally come down.

The Frequency

Beneath the slowed vocal sits a fixed carrier at 123.5 Hz — low, near the bottom of the speaking voice, felt less in the ear than in the belly and the pelvic floor, the seat of water in the body. Split into two tones a hair apart, it produces a binaural beat of 2.5 Hz: delta, the slow floor beneath dreaming, the deepest register the nervous system knows. That state is not incidental to this song — it is the whole secret of it. Numbness is a body reaching for delta through the wrong door: it wants the deep surrender where nothing has to be felt, and it settles for a counterfeit that only mutes the surface while the depths stay clenched. The Hippie Sabotage remix already drags everything toward that floor, stretching and submerging the sound until it moves like water. The binaural finishes the descent honestly — 2.5 Hz does not numb the body, it lowers it, all the way down to the real rest the numbing was imitating, the delta depth where a feeling this large can finally be held instead of fled.

Lalita

A voice counts off the ways it gets through the hours since someone left — filling the emptiness and going hungry by turns, out in loud rooms at two in the morning among people it does not know, staying inside a pretend version of things. And over all of it, the same admission on repeat: stay blurred, all the time, or the empty space lands. The remix takes this and pulls it underwater — the voice pitched down and stretched, the beat dragged slow and heavy, everything moving like it is sinking.

Every verse is a different door, and every door opens back onto the same empty room. The body never stops reaching because it cannot bear to arrive. But listen to what is underneath the reaching. The bass has already slowed to the tempo of someone falling asleep, a low tide breathing in and out beneath the whole song. The voice is fleeing the depths; the music is the depths, and it is calm.

That is the play. Numbness is only feeling with the volume turned down — the ache never left, it just went quiet in the dark. And the slow water underneath is quietly delivering the real thing the numbing was a cheap copy of: actual rest, the deep floor where grief is finally safe to lie down. The song thinks it is running from the deep end. The music is the deep end, arriving underneath to catch the body the moment it stops swimming.

Enjoy the body staying high to keep from going under. Under was never the danger. Under is where it finally gets to rest.

The Seven Lenses

BINDU Feel. Every image in the song is a way around it — the food, the empty stomach, the loud rooms at two in the morning, the pretend world, the blur held on all the time. The song is built entirely out of avoidance, which means it orbits, more precisely than any love song could, the one act it will not commit: to feel the absence directly. The word it never says is the thing it is staying high enough not to touch.

GAIA The body is not asking for the substance or the noise. Filling itself until it feels nothing, then emptying until it feels nothing — that is a nervous system yanking the only lever it can find, trying to make an unbearable feeling stop. The need underneath the numbing is not oblivion. It is rest. It is permission to set the ache down for one hour without that meaning the loss is real. The body is exhausted from bracing. What it wants is to be held while it hurts, not to be spared the hurt.

SID The remix drags the tempo down to a slow tidal pulse and lets a low end swell and recede beneath everything. That slow water is what holds. The vocal thrashes from door to door; the bass just breathes, in and out, at something near the rhythm of a body falling asleep. It was pulsing before the reaching started and it keeps pulsing after the voice gives out. The one steady thing in a song about someone who cannot be steady.

ARCH The remix reaches the body by making it heavy. The vocal is pitched down and stretched until the words go soft at the edges — you hear the numbness before you understand the line, because the sound itself is submerged, slurred, underwater. The beat lands late and thick; the reverb pulls every syllable long. The medium is the message: the song does not describe blurring, it blurs, and your body sinks with it. This is water reaching water — felt in the belly, not parsed in the head.

SAKSHI From outside, the song is a loop of substitutions — each verse a new way to fill the same hole, the chorus always returning to the same quiet confession that none of it is working. Slowed to a crawl, the circle becomes visible: this was never a party. It is a vigil. One person moving through the small hours around a single empty room, trying every door, every door opening back onto the same absence. Not hedonism. Grief that has not been allowed to sit down.

KARISHMA The grace is in the confession. The chorus never pretends the numbing works — it says plainly that the height is a strategy, that someone is gone, that this is what is being fled. To name the absence even while running from it is already half of feeling it. And near ninety seconds, where the low end swells and the vocal dissolves into pure stretched tone, the reaching briefly stops and the song just floats — accidentally arriving, for a few bars, at the exact stillness it was chasing through all the wrong doors.

ASHREY The song about staying numb is a body begging for rest it does not know how to ask for. Numbing is a counterfeit of delta — the deep, dreamless surrender where the nervous system finally stops standing guard. The body reaches for oblivion because it is tired of feeling, but what it actually wants is not to stop feeling; it is to stop bracing. And the music delivers the real version of the thing the numbing imitates: slowed to the tempo of letting go, dropped into the low tidal register of water, it becomes the depth itself. The binaural at 2.5 Hz — delta, the floor of sleep — is the answer: not the false floor of numbness but the true one beneath it, where grief can finally be felt because the body is, at last, held. The song thinks staying high is the way out of feeling. The music opens the way through — all the way down.
